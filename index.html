Equations

An equation asserts that two expressions are equal. What changes from one type to the next is just the shape of the unknown.

Definition. An equation is a mathematical statement that asserts the equality of two expressions, separated by an equal sign (=), indicating that both sides hold the same value.
TypeExample
Linear equation4x-7=9
Quadratic equationx^2-5x+6=0
Absolute value equation|x-4|=5
Exponential equation2^x=16
Example — solve 4x-7=9

Add 7 to both sides: 4x=16

Divide by 4: x=4

Example — solve x^2-5x+6=0

Factor: (x-2)(x-3)=0

Set each factor to zero: x-2=0 \text{ or } x-3=0

Solutions: x=2 \text{ or } x=3

Example — solve |x-4|=5

Split into two cases: x-4=5 \text{ or } x-4=-5

Solve each: x=9 \text{ or } x=-1

Example — solve 2^x=16

Express 16 as a power of 2: 2^x=2^4

Same base ⟹ equal exponents: x=4

2

Quadratic equations

A quadratic equation contains a variable whose highest degree is 2.

General form: ax^2+bx+c=0, where a\neq 0 and a, b, c are constants.
a

Factorization

Best when the trinomial factors neatly.

b

Completing the square

Use for vertex form or to derive the formula.

c

Quadratic formula

Always works — the fallback method.

a) Factorization method

Find two numbers that multiply to ac and add to b, then split the middle term and factor.

Example — solve x^2+5x+6=0

Factor: (x+2)(x+3)=0

Set each factor to zero: x+2=0 \text{ or } x+3=0

Solutions: x=-2 \text{ or } x=-3

b) Completing the square method

Rewrite the equation in the form (x+p)^2=q, then take the square root of both sides.

Example — solve x^2+6x+5=0

Move the constant: x^2+6x=-5

Add \left(\dfrac{6}{2}\right)^2=9 to both sides: x^2+6x+9=4

Factor the left side: (x+3)^2=4

Take square roots: x+3=\pm2 \Rightarrow x=-1 \text{ or } x=-5

Example — solve {-}x^2+3x+5=0 by completing the square

Multiply by −1 first: x^2-3x-5=0 \Rightarrow x^2-3x=5

Add \left(\dfrac{-3}{2}\right)^2=\dfrac94 to both sides: x^2-3x+\dfrac94=5+\dfrac94=\dfrac{29}{4}

Factor: \left(x-\dfrac32\right)^2=\dfrac{29}{4}

Take square roots: x=\dfrac32\pm\dfrac{\sqrt{29}}{2}

c) Quadratic formula

x=\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a} — works for any quadratic equation and gives the roots directly.
Example — solve 2x^2+3x-5=0

Identify: a=2,\ b=3,\ c=-5

Apply the formula: x=\dfrac{-3\pm\sqrt{3^2-4(2)(-5)}}{2(2)}=\dfrac{-3\pm\sqrt{49}}{4}=\dfrac{-3\pm7}{4}

Solutions: x=1 \text{ or } x=-\dfrac52

Choosing the right method — use factorization if the trinomial factors easily; completing the square when you need vertex form or want to derive the quadratic formula; and the quadratic formula for general cases, especially when factoring looks difficult.

Discriminant and nature of roots

The discriminant tells you what the roots will look like before you even solve for them.

For ax^2+bx+c=0, the discriminant is D=b^2-4ac — the expression under the square root in the quadratic formula.

D > 0 — two distinct real roots

D = 0 — one repeated (double) root

D < 0 — two complex roots (conjugate pair), no real solutions

Example — discriminant & roots of 2x^2-4x-6=0

a=2,\ b=-4,\ c=-6

D=(-4)^2-4(2)(-6)=16+48=64\ \ (D>0)

Roots: x=\dfrac{4\pm\sqrt{64}}{4}=\dfrac{4\pm8}{4} \Rightarrow x=3 \text{ or } x=-1

Example — discriminant & root of x^2-6x+9=0

a=1,\ b=-6,\ c=9

D=(-6)^2-4(1)(9)=36-36=0\ \ (D=0)

Root: x=\dfrac{-b}{2a}=\dfrac{6}{2}=3 (double root)

Example — discriminant & roots of x^2+4x+8=0

a=1,\ b=4,\ c=8

D=4^2-4(1)(8)=16-32=-16\ \ (D<0)

Roots: x=\dfrac{-4\pm\sqrt{-16}}{2}=\dfrac{-4\pm4i}{2}=-2\pm2i

Relationship between roots and coefficients

You don't need to solve a quadratic to know the sum and product of its roots — the coefficients already tell you.

For ax^2+bx+c=0 with roots \alpha and \beta:

Sum of roots: \alpha+\beta=-\dfrac{b}{a}    Product of roots: \alpha\beta=\dfrac{c}{a}
IdentityFormula
Sum of squares\alpha^2+\beta^2=(\alpha+\beta)^2-2\alpha\beta
Square of the difference(\alpha-\beta)^2=(\alpha+\beta)^2-4\alpha\beta
Sum of cubes\alpha^3+\beta^3=(\alpha+\beta)\left[(\alpha+\beta)^2-3\alpha\beta\right]
Example — find SOR and POR for 3x^2+5x-2=0

a=3,\ b=5,\ c=-2

Sum of roots: \alpha+\beta=-\dfrac{5}{3}

Product of roots: \alpha\beta=\dfrac{-2}{3}

Example — find SOR and POR for 4x^2-12x+9=0

a=4,\ b=-12,\ c=9

Sum of roots: \alpha+\beta=-\dfrac{-12}{4}=3

Product of roots: \alpha\beta=\dfrac{9}{4}

Forming a quadratic equation from its roots

If a quadratic equation has roots \alpha and \beta, its general form is:

x^2-(\text{SOR})x+(\text{POR})=0
Example — roots are twice the sum and twice the product of the roots of x^2-4x+3=0

For x^2-4x+3=0: \alpha+\beta=4,\ \ \alpha\beta=3

New roots: 2(\alpha+\beta)=8 and 2(\alpha\beta)=6

New equation: x^2-(8+6)x+(8\times6)=x^2-14x+48=0

Example — if \alpha,\beta are roots of x^2-6x+9=0, find the equation with roots \alpha^2, \beta^2

\alpha+\beta=6,\ \ \alpha\beta=9

Sum of new roots: \alpha^2+\beta^2=(\alpha+\beta)^2-2\alpha\beta=36-18=18

Product of new roots: \alpha^2\beta^2=(\alpha\beta)^2=81

New equation: x^2-18x+81=0

Example — the roots of 2x^2-3x+1=0 are \alpha,\beta. Find \alpha^2+\beta^2, (\alpha-\beta)^2 and \alpha^3+\beta^3

\alpha+\beta=\dfrac32,\ \ \alpha\beta=\dfrac12

\alpha^2+\beta^2=\left(\dfrac32\right)^2-2\left(\dfrac12\right)=\dfrac94-1=\dfrac54

(\alpha-\beta)^2=\left(\dfrac32\right)^2-4\left(\dfrac12\right)=\dfrac94-2=\dfrac14

\alpha^3+\beta^3=\dfrac32\left[\dfrac94-\dfrac32\right]=\dfrac32\times\dfrac34=\dfrac98

5

Inequalities

Instead of asking "what value makes both sides equal", an inequality asks "for what values is one side bigger?"

An inequality is a mathematical statement that compares two expressions using >,\ <,\ \geq,\ \leq to show that one is greater than, less than, or equal-and-greater/less than the other.

Linear inequalities

Remember — flip the inequality sign whenever you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number.
Example — solve 2x+5>15

2x>10 \Rightarrow x>5

Example — solve 5-2z\geq1

-2z\geq-4

Divide by −2 and flip the sign: z\leq2

Solving quadratic inequalities

Quadratic inequalities can be solved using any of three methods:

1

Real number line

Factorise, plot the roots, test the sign of each interval.

2

Graphical method

Sketch the parabola and shade where it satisfies the inequality.

3

Table of signs

Tabulate the sign of each factor across every interval.

Example — solve x^2+2x-24<0 (graphical method)

Find where y=x^2+2x-24 crosses zero: (x+6)(x-4)=0 \Rightarrow x=-6 \text{ or } x=4

Since the parabola opens upward, it is below the x-axis (negative) between the roots.

Solution: -6

Example — solve x^2-9x+14\geq0 (graphical method)

Find the roots: (x-2)(x-7)=0 \Rightarrow x=2 \text{ or } x=7

Since the parabola opens upward, it is above or on the x-axis outside the roots.

Solution: x\leq2 \text{ or } x\geq7

Solving rational inequalities

Steps: rewrite with 0 on the right, combine into a single fraction (never cross-multiply variable terms), factorise the numerator and denominator, then apply the real-number-line or table-of-signs method and write the solution set in interval notation.
Example — solve \dfrac{2x+1}{x-4}<1

Bring everything to one side: \dfrac{2x+1}{x-4}-1<0

Combine over a common denominator: \dfrac{(2x+1)-(x-4)}{x-4}<0 \Rightarrow \dfrac{x+5}{x-4}<0

Critical points: x=-5 (numerator) and x=4 (denominator, excluded)

Testing each interval shows the expression is negative only between the critical points.

Solution: -5

6

Absolute values

The absolute value strips away the sign and leaves only "how far from zero".

|x| = \begin{cases} x & \text{if } x\geq0 \\ -x & \text{if } x<0 \end{cases} — always non-negative.
Example — solve |x-3|=7

Split into two cases: x-3=7 \text{ or } x-3=-7

Solutions: x=10 \text{ or } x=-4

Example — solve |x+2|<5

Remove the absolute value as a double inequality: -5

Subtract 2 from all sides: -7

Two flavours — |x| < a becomes a squeeze -a < x < a, while |x| > a splits apart into two branches x < -a \text{ or } x > a. Mixing these up is the most common mistake with absolute value inequalities.
